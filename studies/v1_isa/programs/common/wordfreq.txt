The machine was built on a kitchen table, one chip at a time. First came the
clock, a small crystal and a counter, and then the registers, and then the
ALU, which took a whole weekend and most of a box of wire. When the first
program ran, it printed a single letter, and the letter was wrong. The second
program printed the right letter, but only once. The third program printed
the whole alphabet, and that was the night the machine became a computer.

A computer is not the chips. A computer is the programs, and the programs are
the people who write them. The machine on the table did not care what it ran.
It fetched a word, it decoded the word, it did what the word said, and then
it fetched the next word. It did this a million times a second, and it never
got bored, and it never asked why.

The people who wrote the programs asked why all the time. Why is the stack so
small? Why does the carry flag change when I add one? Why is the multiply so
slow? Each question became a note, and each note became a change, and each
change made a new machine. The new machine ran the old programs a little
faster, and the people wrote new programs, and the new programs asked new
questions.

That is how it goes with machines and with programs. The machine is never
finished, because the programs are never finished, and the programs are never
finished, because the people are never finished asking. One day the table
will be cleared and the machine will go in a box. But the programs will still
be there, written down, waiting for the next machine to run them, one word at
a time.
